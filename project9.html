Synthetic Data Generation and Training for LLM

A teacher model writes children's stories. A filter cleans them. Small student models learn from a mix of a little real data and the synthetic stories. Then we check, on real stories the students never saw, whether it helped. Everything runs on a CPU.

Question: when real data is scarce, does teacher-written data improve a small student at the same training budget, and which controls (filtering, ratio, diversity) matter?

Start here: the basic ideas

Synthetic data is training text written by a model instead of by people. A bigger teacher writes examples; a smaller student learns from them as if they were real. It is cheap, but the teacher's mistakes and habits come along, and the text can be less varied than real writing.

a student who only has five textbooks can read them over and over, or also read practice sheets a tutor wrote. The sheets are not as good as new textbooks, but they are better than a sixth re-read.

Test loss measures how surprised the student is by each word of a real story it has never seen (in nats per token; lower is better). A drop of 0.04 sounds tiny, but it is the same size as the gap between seeds of a noisy system, so we need careful statistics to trust it.

5,000 real stories→prompts→teacher writes 15,000→9-stage filter→mixtures, 6M tokens each→27 students→test on real stories

How much synthetic data?

Pick a synthetic share. With a fixed budget, more synthetic tokens means the 5,000 real stories are repeated fewer times.

The sweet spot is 25–50%. Past that, the student learns the teacher's style instead of real stories. Synthetic-only is worse than real-only.

The 16 phases

1–2

Setup and hypotheses

fix everything except the data

What we did

A new project9 folder with its own environment (PyTorch 2.14.1 CPU, transformers 5.22.2), an environment report with sha256 hashes of every input, and 11 written predictions with the exact test for each, before any student existed.

Why

If you only decide afterwards what counts as success, you can always find a story that fits. Controls (same model, budget, optimiser, schedule, checkpoint rule) make sure the data is the only thing that varies.

Decision

Make real data truly scarce: 5,000 stories and a 6M-token budget. With all 2 million TinyStories available, synthetic data could never matter.

3

Seed and test data

keep the measuring stick clean

What we did

5,000 seed stories (0.97M tokens), 40,000 fresh real stories for the reference, 500 validation, 2,000 test and 300 prompt stories, from separate sources with zero overlap (checked by hash).

Problem found: 5.5% of real TinyStories contain encoding debris like â€™. The synthetic filter removes such text, so every comparison is repeated on ASCII-only test stories.
4

The teacher

good, licensed, affordable, clean

What we did

TinyStories-33M (MIT licence, ~6× the student) writes the stories. A pilot of 576 stories was read by an AI annotator (Claude, not a human): fluent and on topic (relevance 2/2 for 90 of 100), but often illogical (logic errors in 66 of 100).

teacher, prompt in blueOnce upon a time, in a sunny meadow, there was a curious fox named Mia. She wanted to find a hidden treasure. … the kind of frame prompt the teacher continues.

Decision

Generation was too slow at first (378 tokens/s). Chunked generation, dropping finished stories, and top-k instead of top-p brought it to ~730 tokens/s: about an hour per 15,000 stories.

5–6

Prompts and generation

diversity goes in through the prompt

What we did

Two prompt types: the first sentence of a real seed story (open), and random frames built from 40 names, 20 places, 40 goals (frame). 5,000 prompts × 3 candidates = 15,000 stories per pool. Three pools: main (temperature 1.0), low temperature (0.6), and "Once upon a time," for every prompt.

ask a hundred people "tell me a story" and you get the same few stories. Ask "tell me a story about a shy turtle at the beach who wants to learn to sing" and you get a hundred different ones.
7–9

Parse, filter, deduplicate

thresholds set on real stories

Try it

Switch filter rules on and off for the main pool (15,000 stories). Each removed story is counted under the first rule it failed.

Why

Each rule removes what is rarer than the worst ~1% of real stories, so the filter removes the unusual, not what we happen to dislike. On real seed stories the rules reject 2%.

Surprise: near-duplicate search (MinHash, Jaccard ≥ 0.5) found 0 duplicates in 45,000 stories. Even the 3 stories from the same prompt share only ~5% of their 5-word phrases. Contamination with the test set: 0. So the "no dedup" ablation had byte-identical data and was not trained.

11–12

Mixtures and training

same 6M tokens for everyone

What we did

12 mixtures; when a source is too small it is repeated in reshuffled epochs. 27 students from scratch (4 layers, width 256, 5.3M parameters), about 10 minutes each. The learning rate came from a pilot on validation data: 5e-4, 1e-3, 2e-3 had their best at the edge, so 4e-3 and 8e-3 were added; 4e-3 won.

Finding: no run overfit. Even real only, which sees its 5,000 stories 6 times, was still improving at the last step. The data was scarce in variety, not yet in memorisation.
13

Evaluation

real held-out stories only

What we did

Test loss on 2,000 real stories, compared per story with a bootstrap (10,000 resamples), plus the seed-pairing check. Generations from 300 held-out openings, scored by an independent model (SmolLM2-135M).

Result

Synthetic data helps (−0.043) and recovers 52% of what new real data gives. Generations of synthetic-trained students score as more fluent, but the scorer also prefers the dullest students, so test loss stays the main evidence. No student copies a training story.

15

Errors

what goes wrong, read blind

What we did

120 continuations (20 prompts × 5 students + real) were shuffled with the system hidden and rated by an AI annotator (Claude, not a human).

Result

Real stories: coherence 1.8 of 2. Every student: about 0.1. 90 of 100 student continuations lose the plot: characters swap names, events do not follow. A 0.04 gain in loss is real but invisible to a reader at this size. Students trained on more synthetic data finish more stories and say "the moral of the story" a bit more often, a teacher habit.

16

Cost and reproducibility

is it worth it?

Teacher: ~1 CPU-hour per pool, 13,951 accepted stories per hour. Filter: ~6 s (165 s with the judge). Students: 27 runs, 4.9 CPU-hours. One teacher hour bought 52% of the gain of 6M new real tokens. All seeds are fixed; INSTRUCTIONS.md lists every command.

Lessons learned (useful for any ML project)

  1. Synthetic data is a partial substitute. It helps when real data runs out, about half as much as more real data.
  2. Variety beats repetition. More distinct stories helped; lower temperature reduced variety.
  3. Calibrate filters on real data and record why every item was removed.
  4. Check that a "nothing found" is real. Dedup found 0 duplicates; a direct check confirmed it was not a bug.
  5. Check seeds, not only test examples. A confident story-level interval can hide seed-to-seed noise.
  6. Fluency scores can reward dull text. Keep the main metric on real held-out data.
  7. Look at the outputs. Numbers said "better"; reading said "all still incoherent".
